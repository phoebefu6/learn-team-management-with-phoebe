Remote, async and the person who knows everything

Three risks decide whether a team that works on paper keeps working: the links between people thinning when nobody shares a room, decisions stalling when nobody shares a clock, and the whole team depending on one person who knows the thing nobody else does. Tonight we read what one large study found when a whole company went remote, separate remote from async, write the defaults that keep a distributed team deciding, put a shield in front of the interrupts, and write Northline's plan for the day Priya leaves, then check it on the bench.

Part 1 · covers remote work and collaboration networks

When nobody shares a room, the links between groups thin first

5 min live

In 2021, Longqi Yang and colleagues published a study in Nature Human Behaviour of what happened to collaboration inside one large technology company when it moved to firm-wide remote work in 2020. Its own summary page reports that groups became less interconnected and more siloed, that time spent with cross-group connections fell by about 25 percent of the pre-pandemic level, and that people added fewer new collaborators and shed fewer old ones.

LiveWhat the study found, and how far it reaches3 min▶

The summary page also reports that total meeting and call hours fell by about 5 percent while asynchronous communication, email and instant messaging, rose. The authors read the shift as making it harder to acquire and share new information across the network. Secondary coverage reports the sample as 61,182 US employees in the first half of 2020; that figure was not read in the paper itself.

How far it reaches: one company, one sudden and involuntary move, during a pandemic. It describes what happens to the links between groups when nobody chose remote work or redesigned for it, not what a team that plans for distance can do. For a team lead the useful finding is narrow and strong: inside-team links survive distance; bridges to other teams decay unless someone maintains them on purpose.

Self-studyNorthline's bridges3 min▶

Northline's bridges are its stakeholders and its neighbours: merchandising and finance, the store-systems team that fixes source data, and marketing analytics. Before the hybrid week, half of those links ran through corridor conversations Tom and Sam had near the merchandising desks. Those conversations do not happen on the two remote days, and nobody wrote down that they were part of the job.

Ana's move is to name each bridge in the charter, give it an owner, and give it a written rhythm: a fortnightly note to merchandising on what changed in the numbers, and a standing slot in the store-systems team's intake. A bridge that has an owner and a rhythm survives distance; one that relied on a hallway does not.

Real world

The analyst who used to "just know". A common pattern in hybrid teams: one analyst always seemed to know what the business would ask next week. After the move to remote work, the team's forecasts kept missing context. Nobody had lost skill. The analyst had lost the coffee-queue conversations that carried the context, and nobody had replaced them with anything written.

Part 2 · covers remote against async

Remote is about where people are; async is about when they decide

5 min live

A remote team can still run every decision in a live call, and an office team can run most of them in writing. The question that matters is not where people sit but whether the team can make a decision without everyone present at the same moment. When it cannot, every decision waits for the one hour all calendars agree on, and the people outside that hour stop being part of decisions at all.

LiveFive async defaults3 min▶

These are common practice in distributed teams rather than findings from a study, and each is small enough to adopt this week.

1. Write it before you meet. A meeting starts from a written proposal, and anything a meeting decides is written in the decision log the same day. 2. Decide in writing by default. Use the decision-rights table from session 2: the decider is named on the proposal, the comment window has an end time, and silence by the deadline is not a veto. 3. Keep a few overlap hours. Two or three hours a day when everyone is reachable, for the conversations that genuinely need a voice. 4. Status is written, not spoken. A short written update replaces a status meeting, which also removes a line from session 3's ritual budget. 5. Every bridge has a written rhythm, from Part 1.

Self-studyWhat stays synchronous2 min▶

Some things lose too much in writing: a disagreement that has started to turn personal (session 5 routes the people part to a one-to-one, taught in Performance Management, hard conversations ↗), a brainstorm where ideas need to build on each other fast, and the first weeks of a new team member. Running those meetings well is Facilitation ↗. The default flips, not the whole calendar: write first, and meet when writing has shown that a meeting is needed.

Part 3 · covers key-person risk and interrupt load

The person who knows everything, and the person who answers everything

5 min live

The bus factor, or truck factor, is the smallest number of people whose loss would leave the team unable to keep some part of its work running. A 2016 study by Avelino, Passos, Hora and Valente estimated it from code authorship for 133 popular open-source projects on GitHub and found that 65 percent had a truck factor of two or less. On Northline's declared map the bus factor is 1, and the bench showed what that means: when Priya leaves, the pipeline has nobody.

LiveRaising the bus factor is a plan with a date3 min▶

A bus factor rises only when a second person can actually run the area, which takes their time. That is the trade the bench makes visible: in design 4, after Jun has learned the pipeline, the bus factor is 2, and pressing the break button stops nothing; the pipeline then rests on Jun alone, a bus factor of 1, which is survivable for the weeks it takes to train the next person. The tools are plain: pairing on real work, a runbook the second person writes while learning, and rotating the area's on-call so knowledge has to move.

The Avelino study's definition of the truck factor, the minimal number of developers who would have to leave before a project is incapacitated, is estimated from who wrote which files. The bench's version is simpler, the smallest number of knowers over the areas on a map the team declares, and it shares the study's limit: knowing who touched something is not the same as being able to run it.

Self-studyThe interrupt shield3 min▶

Session 3 showed, from Mark, Gudith and Klocke's 2008 study, that interrupted people keep their speed by paying in stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. On the bench, Ana carries the most interrupts on the team and has the lowest focus in three of the four designs. A shield routes every incoming request to one person for the week, who triages, answers what they can and queues the rest for the weekly priority decision. The other six keep their focus blocks.

Rotate it weekly, including through the lead, and the shield doubles as cross-training: the person on shield sees requests from every area and learns who to ask. It is common practice in operations and data teams, not a measured result, and it moves interrupt minutes rather than removing them. The gain is that six people have unbroken time, and the cost lands on one person who knows it is their week.

Where the departure itself belongs. How to handle a resignation, a handover conversation or an exit interview with the person leaving is taught in Performance Management ↗. These pages are about making sure the team keeps working whoever leaves.

Build-along

Northline's key-person plan, checked on the bench

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Ana writes a plan for every area that rests on one person, starting with the pipeline (constructed). Write the same plan for the fewest-dots row on your own knowledge map from session 4. The plan has a target, an owner, a date and a check, and the check is the same break button the bench uses.

Pick the area and the second person. Start from the fewest-dots row. The second person should be someone whose own area is already shared, so learning does not create a new single point.

★ Step 1 · area and learnerArea: the sales and stock pipeline (20% of weekly work) Knower: Priya (bus factor 1) Learner: Kofi (shares infrastructure with Priya already; his other area, data quality, has 3 knowers) Target: bus factor 2 on the pipeline by the end of week 8

Budget the time, from the rhythm. Learning is work and goes in the session 3 budget. Pairing hours come out of both people's focus time, so something else gives way, and the decider from session 2 says what.

★ Step 2 · the timePairing: 4 hours a week, Priya and Kofi, weeks 1-6 (constructed estimate) Gives way: the dashboard backlog slips two weeks Decided by: Ana, as owner of the weekly priority order (decision-rights table, row 1)

Pair on real work, learner driving. Watching does not transfer the ability to run something. Kofi runs each pipeline change with Priya beside him, starting with the weekly load and ending with a failure recovery.

★ Step 3 · pairing orderWeeks 1-2: weekly load, with Priya watching Weeks 3-4: a schema change on a test copy Weeks 5-6: a staged failure and recovery Each week: Kofi writes what he could not do alone

The learner writes the runbook. The person who did not know is the one who knows what is missing from the page. The expert reviews; the runbook lives where the team's decision log lives.

★ Step 4 · runbookWritten by: Kofi Reviewed by: Priya Sections: what runs when / how to tell it failed / the five most common failures and their fixes / who to call upstream (store-systems team intake) Lives in: the team wiki, linked from the charter line 3

Run the check: the expert takes a week off. The plan is proved when the learner runs the area for a week alone. If something stops, it goes back into the runbook and the week is repeated.

★ Step 5 · the checkWeek 8: Priya on leave; Kofi runs the pipeline alone Pass: Monday 09:00 numbers delivered, no call to Priya Then: update the knowledge map; bus factor on the pipeline = 2. Next row: infrastructure, if Priya moves on it rests on Kofi alone (the bench shows it)

Put a shield in front of the plan. Pairing weeks fail when the pair is interrupted. Add the rotation from Part 3 and keep Priya and Kofi off it during weeks 1 to 6.

★ Step 6 · shield rotaRotation: Ana, Sam, Tom, Lena, Wei (weekly) Priya and Kofi rejoin from week 7 Shield's job: triage every request, answer what it can, queue the rest for Monday's priority decision

Check it on the bench. Open session 4, choose design 2 and press the break button: the pipeline stops. Choose design 4, where a second person has learned the pipeline, and press it again: nothing stops, and the bus factor reads 2 to 1. That is the before and after this plan is for, in a team of seven instead of nine.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does the 2021 study of firm-wide remote work best support?

The summary page reports fewer bridges between groups, about 25 percent less cross-group time, and more asynchronous communication. It does not measure productivity, and it describes one company's unplanned move, so the safe lesson is about maintaining bridges, not about where to work.

2 · Northline's three remote members hear about decisions a day late. What is the best first change?

The problem is when decisions happen, not where people sit. Async defaults let everyone take part in their own hours; a daily all-hands call makes every decision wait for one slot, and D removes the team from its own decisions.

3 · When is Northline's pipeline bus factor actually 2?

A bus factor counts people who can keep the area running, not people who have seen it. The truck-factor study's own caveat applies: touching something is not the same as being able to run it. The check week is the proof.
