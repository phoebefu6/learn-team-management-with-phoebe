The team's rhythm and what it costs

Every stand-up, planning session, retro and review is paid for in the one currency a team cannot print: its members' hours. Tonight we put Northline's rituals on a budget sheet and price each one in person-hours, test the rule that decides whether a ritual earns its place (it has to replace coordination that was happening anyway), read what the research on interrupts actually found rather than what gets quoted, and write a ritual budget the team can defend line by line.

About the numbers. Every hour figure on this page comes from the team-size bench you open in session 4. Its rules are stated in the open (a 40-hour week, the ritual formulas below, a fixed cost per communication path) and run on Northline, a constructed team. Part 1 · covers the rhythm as a budget

Every ritual is a line in a budget

5 min live

A ritual looks small on one calendar: fifteen minutes here, an hour every other Friday there. On the team's ledger it is multiplied by everyone in the room. Northline's proposed set (a daily stand-up, weekly planning, a fortnightly retro and a fortnightly review) costs each person 210 minutes, 3.5 hours, a week on the bench's rules. Across seven people that is 3.5 h x 7 = 24.5 person-hours a week, more than half of one person's whole week, before a single decision has been made in any of those meetings.

LivePrice each ritual: cadence x length x attendees3 min▶

Write each ritual as a row. Cadence, length, who attends, then minutes per person per week. The bench uses four formulas for a team of n people: stand-up 5 x (5 + 2n) minutes a week (five days, a fixed opening plus about two minutes a person), planning 30 + 5n, retro 60 minutes fortnightly (30 a week), review 40 minutes fortnightly (20 a week). For Northline's seven: 95 + 65 + 30 + 20 = 210 minutes, 3.5 hours each.

Multiply by the people in the room. That is the number a calendar hides. Stand-up 95 x 7 = 665 minutes, 11.1 person-hours. Planning 65 x 7 = 455 minutes, 7.6. Retro 30 x 7 = 210 minutes, 3.5. Review 20 x 7 = 140 minutes, 2.3. Together, 24.5 person-hours a week (constructed team, modelled rules).

Notice which lines grow. Stand-up and planning have n in the formula: every person adds time to everyone's meeting. Retro and review do not. Add Mo and Jun in session 4 and the stand-up becomes 5 x (5 + 18) = 115 minutes, planning 30 + 45 = 75, and the set goes from 210 to 240 minutes, 4.0 h each, across nine people instead of seven.

Self-studyWhat each ritual is for, in one line3 min▶

A ritual without a one-line job description drifts into a status meeting. Write the job before the invite:

  • Stand-up: surface blockers today so nobody waits until tomorrow to ask for help.
  • Planning: agree what the team works on this week, so individual "what next?" questions have an answer.
  • Retro: change one thing about how the team works, based on the last two weeks.
  • Review: show finished work to the people it is for, and hear what they need next.

How to run each of these meetings well, from the agenda to the closing decision, is facilitation, taught in Facilitation ↗. Using AI to summarise and synthesise meetings is in AI Leadership: meetings and synthesis ↗. Here the question is only which meetings the team runs and what the set costs.

Real world

The stand-up that grew to 40 minutes. A common pattern: a stand-up starts at fifteen minutes with six people. The team grows, nobody changes the format, and every new person adds their round of updates plus the side discussions their work starts. A year later it runs forty minutes daily, people join with cameras off and work in another window, and the blockers it exists to surface come up in direct messages afterwards. The formula 5 + 2n predicted the growth; the fix is to price it at each hire and change the shape (split it, cap updates at blockers only, or move updates into writing) before it becomes the most expensive line in the budget.

Part 2 · covers replace, do not add

A ritual pays only when it replaces coordination that was happening anyway

5 min live

A team without rituals is not a team without coordination. It coordinates ad hoc: a quick call here, a long thread there, a question that interrupts someone's afternoon. The bench prices that too. A ritual set is a trade, not a cost: it is worth 3.5 hours a person only if it removes more than 3.5 hours of coordination that was going to happen anyway. Whether it does depends on how heavy the ad hoc load really was.

LiveThe bench rule, and the half hour it buys3 min▶

Without rituals. On the bench, each communication path costs each of its two people 60 minutes a week when coordination is ad hoc. In a team of 7 everyone sits on 6 paths, so 6 x 60 = 360 minutes, 6.0 hours of coordination each. After interrupts, Northline's mean focus time is 31.4 hours a person.

With the ritual set. The rituals cost 3.5 hours each, and because blockers, priorities and updates now have a home, each path drops to 20 minutes: 6 x 20 = 120 minutes, 2.0 hours. Rituals plus coordination is 5.5 hours, against 6.0 before. Mean focus rises from 31.4 to 31.9 hours, half an hour a person (modelled on a constructed team).

When the ad hoc load is lighter. Run the same team at 30 minutes per path and coordination without rituals is 3.0 hours; focus is 34.4. Adding the ritual set still leaves 31.9, so the set now costs 34.4 - 31.9 = 2.5 hours a person. Same meetings, opposite verdict. The ritual set does not create focus time; it can only convert ad hoc coordination into scheduled coordination, and that is worth doing only when there was a lot of ad hoc coordination to convert.

Self-studyEstimate your team's real ad hoc load4 min▶

The 60-minute and 30-minute figures are bench rules, not measurements. Your team has its own number, and you can estimate it in a week without any tool:

  • Count syncs for one normal week. Ask each person to tally every unplanned call, thread longer than a few messages and "got a minute?" conversation with a teammate, with a rough length.
  • Sort them by what they were for. Blockers, "what should I work on next", updates someone needed, decisions, help with a problem. Each kind maps to the ritual that could absorb it, or to none.
  • Compare the total with the ritual cost. If the week's ad hoc coordination is well above what the rituals would cost, a set will pay. If it is already light, adding rituals adds hours.
  • Keep what no ritual would replace. Pairing on a hard problem is ad hoc and valuable. The goal is fewer accidental syncs, not zero conversation.

The count is noisy and one week can be unusual. That is fine: the decision needs an order of magnitude, not a precise figure.

Real world

The status meeting that replaced nothing. A familiar pattern: a team already posts written updates in a shared channel every afternoon, and someone adds a weekly status meeting "so we are all aligned". Each person reads out what they already wrote. No coordination moved into the meeting, because none was left outside it. The meeting is pure cost, multiplied by everyone in the room, every week. The test is one question per ritual: what ad hoc coordination stopped happening when we started this? If the answer is nothing, it goes.

Part 3 · covers interrupts and focus

Interrupts are paid for later, in stress

5 min live

Interrupts are the coordination nobody scheduled. On the bench they are seeded draws, and they land unevenly: over 12 modelled weeks Ana averages 279.2 minutes of interrupts a week and Sam 232.1, while Wei gets 84.6 and Lena 90.8. The lead and the person stakeholders know best absorb the most. What interrupts actually cost a person is a narrower question than the famous figures suggest, and the study usually behind them found something different.

LiveWhat Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008) found, read at source3 min▶

The finding. In a lab study at CHI 2008, Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke interrupted people during an email task. People who were interrupted completed the interrupted tasks in less time, with no difference in quality. They paid for it in more stress, higher frustration, more time pressure and more effort. Whether the interruption was related to the task or not made no difference.

What it means for a team. An interrupt does not show up as slower or worse work on the day. People compensate by working faster, and the cost lands on them as strain. A lead who looks only at output will see nothing wrong while the most-interrupted people on the team carry the bill.

How far it reaches. One lab study, an email task, participants mostly German university students. It is a careful result about short-term speed and reported stress, not a measurement of a working team over months.

Self-studyTwo figures that are not from this paper, and three ways to protect focus4 min▶

"23 minutes to get back on task." This figure is widely attributed to Gloria Mark's research, but it does not appear in the 2008 paper read for this page. Its source was not verified, so it is not taught here as a fact.

"Multitasking costs 40 percent of productivity." Quoted everywhere, traced to no source read for this page. Not verified, not taught.

Protecting focus is a design choice, not a personal habit. Three moves that change where interrupts land:

  • Team-wide focus blocks. The same hours for everyone, no meetings, no pings except for incidents. A block only one person keeps is a block everyone else books over.
  • A request intake. Stakeholder requests go to one queue, triaged by one person on rotation, instead of to whoever answers first. The interrupt still happens; it lands on one person on purpose. Session 6 builds the rotation.
  • Batched questions. Non-urgent questions to a teammate go into a thread they answer at a set time, or into the stand-up, rather than a "got a minute?" mid-afternoon.

Stress here means workload: hours, interrupts and who carries them. Nothing on this page is medical advice; the body side belongs to the Stress Control course planned on the Learn with Phoebe hub ↗.

Real world

The most helpful person becomes the router. A common pattern: one analyst answers messages fastest, so stakeholders learn to message them first, and teammates do the same. Within months they spend their days forwarding questions and context to the right person, their own work slips to evenings, and their output still looks fine, exactly the pattern the 2008 study describes, at a larger scale. Nobody decided this. An intake with a rotation makes the same job visible, shared and bounded.

Re-price at every hire. The budget is right for seven people. When Mo and Jun join, the same four rituals cost 240 minutes each, 4.0 h, and the team has 36 paths instead of 21. Session 4 runs exactly that on the bench.

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Planning on the bench costs 30 + 5n minutes a week per person. What does it cost Northline's team of 7 each week?

30 + 5 x 7 = 65 minutes per person, and seven people sit in it: 65 x 7 = 455 minutes, 7.6 person-hours. A calendar shows one 65-minute meeting; the team pays it seven times.

2 · On the bench, when does Northline's ritual set cost focus time instead of saving it?

The set costs 3.5 h a person and cuts each path from its ad hoc cost to 20 minutes. At 60 minutes ad hoc that saves 4.0 h and nets +0.5 h; at 30 minutes it saves only 1.0 h, so the set loses 2.5 h. A ritual pays only when it replaces coordination that was happening anyway (modelled, constructed team).

3 · What did Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008) actually find about interrupted work?

The lab study (email task, mostly German university students) found people compensated by working faster and paid in strain. The 23-minute figure is not from this paper and the 40 percent claim is not verified; neither is taught as fact.
