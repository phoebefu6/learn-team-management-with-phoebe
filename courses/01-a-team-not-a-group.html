Learn Team Management with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

A team is not a group of people with the same manager

Seven people can share a manager, a budget line and a channel and still not be a team. A team shares an outcome nobody can deliver alone, knows who is in it and who is not, and depends on each other's work every week. Tonight we draw that line, count what every extra person adds in communication paths, look honestly at the claim that there is an ideal team size, name the four shapes a team can take, and write the one-page charter that turns a group into a team on paper first.

Part 1 · covers group against team

A shared outcome, a boundary, and work that depends on each other

5 min live

A group of people with the same manager is a reporting line. Each person can do their job well and the group still delivers nothing together, because there is nothing it delivers together. A team is defined by the work, not the org chart: an outcome that needs several of its members, a clear answer to who is in, and members whose work feeds each other's. Northline today is the first kind, and Ana's job is to make it the second.

LiveThree questions that tell a team from a group3 min▶

Is there an outcome nobody here can deliver alone? "Each person hits their own targets" is a group. "Stakeholders get a weekly sales forecast they trust" needs Priya's pipeline, Kofi's platform, Wei's model and Tom's dashboard, and fails if any one of them slips. That is a team outcome.

Can everyone name who is in? Ask each member to list the team. If the lists differ, the boundary is fuzzy, and a fuzzy boundary means nobody knows who must be in a decision, who gets the stand-up invite, or who covers when someone is away. At Northline, two people list the contractor who left in spring; one lists the stakeholder who sends the most requests.

Does work pass between members every week? Interdependence is the price and the point. If nobody ever needs anyone else's work, there is no team problem to solve, and forcing team rituals on a group of independent specialists only adds meetings.

Self-studyHackman's "real team", and what is only reported3 min▶

J. Richard Hackman's Leading Teams (2002) built its argument on five conditions for team effectiveness: a real team, a compelling direction, an enabling structure, a supportive organisational context, and competent coaching. The first, a real team, is described in summaries as clear boundaries, interdependence among members and some stability of membership. That matches the three questions above. The book itself was not read for these pages, so the list is reported from publisher listings and summaries, and no team-size number attributed to Hackman is repeated here.

The useful part for a team lead is the ordering. Hackman's conditions are mostly about the design of the team, settled before any coaching happens. Coaching individual people is a different craft, taught in Performance Management ↗; these pages stay with the design.

Real world

The "team" that is really a pool. A familiar pattern: a central analytics function of eight people, each assigned to a different business unit, sharing a manager and a weekly status meeting. The status meeting is eight separate updates nobody else needs. It is not a broken team; it is a pool of specialists, and it is better run as one: shared standards, a light rhythm, no pretence of a shared outcome. Calling it a team and adding team rituals is how meetings multiply without anything getting better.

Part 2 · covers size and communication paths

Every person you add brings a line to everyone already there

5 min live

Among n people there are n(n-1)/2 pairs who might need to talk. That is arithmetic, not research. Three people have 3 paths, five have 10, seven have 21, nine have 36: headcount grew threefold and paths twelvefold. Melvin Conway made the point in 1968, writing that the number of possible communication paths "is approximately half the square of the number of people", and argued against "the assumption that adding manpower simply adds to productivity".

LiveNot every path is used, and that is the design question3 min▶

A path is a pair who might need to coordinate, not a pair who must. The count is an upper bound on the coordination a team can generate, and much of team design is deciding which paths to use on purpose (a stand-up that covers all of them in fifteen minutes), which to route (requests go through one intake, not to whoever answers), and which to cut (two sub-teams that only meet at one interface). Session 3 prices the first, session 2 the second, and session 4 shows what the third does to Northline's week.

The count also explains a feeling every lead of a growing team has: the team got two people bigger and somehow everything got slower. Going from 7 to 9 adds 15 paths. Without a change in how the team coordinates, those paths are filled with ad hoc syncs.

Self-study"The ideal team size", and the evidence there is not4 min▶

Dunbar's number. The claim that humans can maintain about 150 stable relationships comes from Robin Dunbar's comparative work on primate brains and group sizes (reported). It is often stretched to company and team design. A 2021 re-analysis by Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind, using the same kind of data with newer statistical methods, estimated mean human group sizes of about 69 to 109 with one method and 16 to 42 with another, with 95 percent intervals of 4 to 520 and 2 to 336, and concluded that "specifying any one number is futile". Either way, the number is about social networks, not about how many people can share a sprint.

Google's Project Aristotle. Google's re:Work guide reports a study of 180 of its teams, sized from 3 to 50 with a median of nine, and lists team size among the factors that were not significantly connected with effectiveness at Google. That is a company's summary of an internal study whose methods and data are not published, so read it as "Google reports", not as a finding about teams in general.

What a lead can use. No source read for these pages supports a single right size. What is solid is the arithmetic: paths grow with the square of headcount, so a bigger team needs more deliberate structure to keep coordination from eating the week. Session 4 makes that concrete with hours.

Part 3 · covers team shapes and Conway's law

What kind of team is this, and who does it serve?

5 min live

Conway's 1968 paper argued that organisations which design systems "are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations". Later writers named that Conway's law. Its practical reading for a team lead: the shape of the team and the shape of what it builds end up the same, so choose the team's shape on purpose. Team Topologies, a practitioner book by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais, offers a vocabulary of four team types and three ways teams interact.

LiveThe four types, and which one Northline is3 min▶

On the Team Topologies site, a stream-aligned team works on a flow of work, usually one segment of the business; a platform team offers an internal product that speeds up stream-aligned teams; an enabling team helps stream-aligned teams overcome obstacles; a complicated subsystem team handles work that needs deep specialist expertise. The three interaction modes are collaboration (working together for a period to discover something), X-as-a-Service (one team provides, another consumes) and facilitating (one team helps and mentors another). The authors also stress that a team can only carry so much cognitive load.

Northline is all four at once. Priya and Kofi run what is really a platform, the pipeline and the infrastructure. Tom and Sam serve a stream of business questions. Wei and Lena do specialist modelling. Nobody chose this, so every path is open, which is exactly the 21-path problem from Part 2. Session 4 tries one split on the bench and shows what it fixes and what it moves.

Self-studyHow much weight to put on a practitioner vocabulary2 min▶

Team Topologies is a practitioner framework, drawn from its authors' consulting experience, not a research finding, and these pages treat it that way: a useful set of names that makes a conversation about team shape faster. The key-concepts page read for these pages does not mention a team size or Conway's law, though the book builds on Conway; anything about the book beyond that page is reported. Conway's own paper, read at source, is an argument with examples from 1968 systems work, not a measured study. Both are good tools for thinking and weak tools for proving.

Build-along

Northline's team charter, on one page

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A charter is the team's definition of itself, short enough to read in two minutes and specific enough to settle an argument. Ana writes Northline's in one sitting with the team (constructed). Write the same page for your own team as you go; if you do not lead one, write it for a team you are in. Sessions 2 and 3 fill in the two lines it points to.

Write the outcome as something only the team can deliver. Use the first question from Part 1. If one person could deliver it alone, it is a job description, not a team outcome. Ana tries three drafts before one passes.

★ Step 1 · outcomeNorthline exists so that merchandising and finance get trusted weekly numbers (sales, stock, forecast) by Monday 09:00, and can ask follow-up questions the same week. Test: can any one of us deliver this alone? no

List the members, and say who is not in. Names, not roles. Then the near-misses: the people who feel like members but are not, so nobody waits for them in a decision.

★ Step 2 · members and boundaryIn: Ana (lead), Priya, Tom, Wei, Sam, Lena, Kofi Not in: the merchandising analyst who sends most requests (a stakeholder: consulted, not a member) the contractor who left in spring (remove from channels) Check: all seven list the same seven names

Write what the team owns. Areas of work, each with a short definition. Keep it to what the team will be held to; vague ownership ("data") is how a team ends up owning everything.

★ Step 3 · what we own- The sales and stock pipeline, from source extracts to tables - The weekly forecast model and its accuracy report - Six standard dashboards (listed in the wiki) - Ad hoc analysis for merchandising and finance, up to the intake limit in session 3

Write what the team does not own, and where it goes instead. This is the line that protects the week. Every "no" carries a destination, so it reads as routing, not refusal.

★ Step 4 · what we do not own- Source system fixes -> the store-systems team - Marketing attribution models -> the marketing analytics team - One-off data pulls for HR -> the HR self-serve report - New dashboards outside the six -> request, prioritised weekly

Agree four to six working agreements. Specific enough to notice when one is broken. Write them with the team, not for it; an agreement the team did not make is a rule.

★ Step 5 · working agreements1. Requests come through the intake, not direct messages 2. Anything that changes a shared table is announced first 3. 09:30-12:30 Tuesday and Thursday is focus time: no meetings 4. A decision made in chat is copied to the decision log 5. Anyone can say "I do not understand" in any meeting

Point to decision rights and the rhythm. Two lines that say "see the table" for now. Session 2 writes the decision-rights table, session 3 the ritual budget.

★ Step 6 · pointersWho decides what: decision-rights table (session 2) Our rhythm: ritual budget (session 3) Review this charter: first Monday of each quarter

Test it on one real argument. Ana picks the last disagreement Northline had: whether to build a dashboard the regional managers asked for. The charter answers it in ten seconds (line 4: outside the six, so it goes to the weekly prioritisation). A charter that cannot settle any recent argument is decoration.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Eight analysts share a manager, each assigned to a different business unit, with no work passing between them. What is the best description?

A team needs an outcome nobody can deliver alone, a boundary, and work that passes between members. Without interdependence there is no team problem to solve, and adding team rituals only adds meetings.

2 · Northline grows from 7 to 9 people. What happens to its communication paths?

n(n-1)/2: 7 x 6 / 2 = 21 and 9 x 8 / 2 = 36. Each new person adds a path to everyone already there, which is why growth that looks small in headcount is large in coordination.

3 · A colleague says "research proves teams should be under 10, and Dunbar's number explains why". What is accurate?

Neither source supports a magic number for work teams. Google's guide lists team size among the factors that did not matter significantly at Google. C overcorrects: the path arithmetic is real, and more people do need more deliberate coordination.
