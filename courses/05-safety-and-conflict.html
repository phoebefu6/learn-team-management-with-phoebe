Part 1 · covers psychological safety at source

Safe enough to say I broke it

6 min live

Psychological safety is easy to misread as niceness. Amy Edmondson's definition is narrower and more useful: "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking". The risk in question is the small, social one of admitting an error, asking a question that makes you look slow, or saying the plan is wrong. What it changes is the route a mistake takes: straight to the person who can fix it, or underground until it breaks something downstream.

LiveWhat Edmondson's 1999 study found, read at source4 min▶

Edmondson studied 51 work teams in a manufacturing company. From the abstract: team psychological safety was associated with learning behaviour. (In plain terms, learning behaviour means the things a team does to find and fix its own mistakes, such as asking for feedback and raising errors; that gloss is ours, since only the abstract was read.) Team efficacy, the belief that the team is capable, was not associated with learning behaviour once safety was taken into account. And learning behaviour mediated between psychological safety and team performance: safety goes with learning, and learning goes with performance.

Three things follow for a team lead. First, the useful word is learning, not happiness: a safe team is one where errors and questions surface. Second, confidence is not the same thing; a team that believes it is excellent can still hide its mistakes. Third, it is one study of 51 teams in one company, and the effect sizes and survey items were not read for these pages, so neither is quoted. Treat it as a strong, specific finding, not a law.

Northline in that light (constructed): Kofi's silence was not laziness. The last time someone broke a table, the incident thread spent twenty messages on who did it. He did the arithmetic every person does: saying it costs me now, staying quiet might cost nobody. Ana's job is to change that arithmetic.

Self-studyThe 2017 meta-analysis, and why causal claims stay cautious3 min▶

Frazier, Fainshmidt, Klinger, Pezeshkan and Vracheva (2017) pooled the research in a meta-analytic review: 136 independent samples, over 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups. That is a large body of work, and it is why psychological safety is taken seriously as a construct rather than a slogan.

One caution comes with it. A state-agency summary reports that most of the included studies were cross-sectional, measuring safety and outcomes at the same moment, so causal conclusions cannot be drawn. That statement is reported from the summary, not read in the paper. Either way, the honest reading for a team lead is: safety and good outcomes travel together in a great many teams; that safety causes them is plausible but not established by this body of evidence. The meta-analysis's effect sizes were not read and are not quoted.

The practical move survives the caution. You do not need proof of causation to decide that a mistake reported on day one is cheaper than the same mistake found by finance on day three.

Self-studyWhat safety is not2 min▶

Not niceness. A team can be warm, polite and unsafe: nobody is rude, and nobody says the model is wrong. Not low standards. Safety is about the cost of speaking up, not the cost of missing a target; a team can hold a hard bar and still make raising a problem cheap. Not the absence of disagreement. A team where nobody disagrees is more often a team where disagreeing feels expensive. Part 3 is about what happens when it does.

Real world

The incident review that named a person. A common pattern: a broken pipeline causes a bad report, the review document puts one engineer's name in the "root cause" line, and the meeting spends its time on who rather than how. The fix ships. The next incident is noticed by the same engineer within an hour and reported a week later, after they have quietly tried to repair it alone. The review taught exactly one lesson, and it was the wrong one: being the person who finds the problem is dangerous.

Part 2 · covers Project Aristotle as a company report

What Google reports, and what kind of evidence it is

4 min live

Most people meet psychological safety through Google's Project Aristotle, usually as a headline. The source is a guide on Google's re:Work site, and it is worth reading directly, because what it says is more modest and more interesting than the retelling. It is also a different kind of evidence from Part 1, and a team lead who can tell the difference is much harder to mislead.

LiveWhat the re:Work guide says2 min▶

Google reports studying 180 of its teams: 115 engineering project teams and 65 sales pods. Effectiveness was rated from evaluations by executives, team leads and team members, and for sales teams, performance against quota. The guide lists five dynamics, in this order: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact. It defines psychological safety using Edmondson's words.

The part people skip is the list of things that were not significantly connected with effectiveness at Google: colocation, consensus-driven decision making, extroversion, individual performance, workload size, seniority, team size and tenure. That list is as useful as the five: it says a team of strong individuals in one room did not, on Google's own data, guarantee anything.

You have already met two of the five. Dependability and structure and clarity are what sessions 2 and 3 built: who decides, what the rhythm is, who owns which work.

Self-studyHow to tier it, and how to say it2 min▶

The guide is a company's summary of an internal study. Its methods and data are not published, so nobody outside can check how teams were sampled, how "effective" was scored, or how the ranking of the five was decided. That does not make it wrong; it makes it a company report. The accurate phrasing is "Google reports", and the accurate scope is "at Google".

The guide also mentions research suggesting smaller teams perform better without giving the source, so it is not repeated here as a finding. Session 1 covers why no source read for these pages supports a single right team size.

A test you can use on any citation: who collected the data, can I see how, and is the claim I heard the claim they made? Edmondson passes all three. The re:Work guide passes the first and third. The retelling fails the third.

Real world

The offsite that taught "safety means be nice". A familiar pattern: a leadership offsite presents "Google found psychological safety matters most", and the team comes back with a norm of never criticising anyone's work in public. Six weeks later, reviews are shorter, problems arrive later, and people who raise them are told they are "not being safe". The norm reversed the idea: safety is the freedom to say the uncomfortable thing about the work, not a rule against saying it.

Part 3 · covers conflict, contested

Task conflict is not settled; relationship conflict is

5 min live

A popular line says "healthy teams argue about the work". The research distinguishes task conflict (disagreement about the work itself: what to build, which method, what comes first) from relationship conflict (friction between people) and process conflict (disagreement about who does what and how). Two meta-analyses nine years apart looked at the same question and did not land in the same place. The honest move is to teach both, and keep the part they agree on.

LiveTwo meta-analyses, two answers on task conflict3 min▶

De Dreu and Weingart (2003). From the abstract: strong negative correlations between relationship conflict and both team performance and member satisfaction, and also between task conflict and both, "instead of the predicted positive". The authors expected disagreement about the work to help. Across the studies they pooled, it did not.

de Wit, Greer and Jehn (2012). A larger meta-analysis of 116 studies and 8,880 groups. From the abstract: it did not find a strong negative association between task conflict and performance, while relationship conflict and process conflict were negatively related to outcomes. A search summary of the abstract reports moderators: task conflict was more positive where it was only weakly tied to relationship conflict, in top management teams, and where performance was measured financially or as decision quality. Those moderators are reported, not read in the paper.

Neither paper is the final word, and these pages do not pick a winner. What a lead can take away does not depend on who is right: relationship conflict goes with worse outcomes in both, and task conflict looks least harmful when it stays separate from the personal kind.

Self-studyA priority fight is a routing problem with a personality costume3 min▶

Tom wants the dashboard backlog first; Wei wants the forecast rebuild first (constructed). Both are reasonable. The fight turned personal because nobody could end it: two peers were trying to settle a question neither of them owns. That is a decision-rights problem, and session 2 already answered it. The "priority order" row in Northline's decision-rights table names one decider, Ana, and who she consults before deciding.

Seen that way, the lead's job is not to referee two personalities. It is to get the disagreement out of the stand-up, written as two options, and in front of the person who decides, by a date. The task disagreement stays useful because it informs the decision; it stops being corrosive because it has an end.

One seam to respect. If the trouble between two people is no longer about any piece of work, that is a conversation with each person, not a team process. Hard conversations with one person belong to Performance Management: hard conversations ↗.

Real world

The team that never argued and shipped the wrong model. A common pattern: a modelling team prides itself on smooth meetings. A junior scientist notices the validation set overlaps the training window, mentions it once in a side message, gets no reply, and lets it go. The model ships with excellent offline scores and underperforms in production for a quarter. Nobody was rude. The team had simply made disagreement expensive enough that the one argument it needed never happened.

Build-along

Northline's priority-conflict protocol and safety check

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Ana writes two things in one sitting (constructed): a short protocol any member can trigger when two priorities collide, and a five-minute safety check for the retro. Write both for your own team as you go. The protocol leans on your session 2 decision-rights table; if you skipped it, write the one row you need now.

Name the conflict as two options on one page. Not "Tom versus Wei" but "forecast first or dashboards first". Each option gets its case in three lines, written by the person who holds it, and the cost of not choosing it. Writing it down is half the cooling.

★ Step 1 · two options, one pageConflict: what Northline builds first this quarter (constructed) Option A - forecast rebuild first (Wei) - finance plans stock from it; current error is rising - 4 weeks of Wei and Lena (constructed estimate) - cost of waiting: another quarter of manual adjustments Option B - dashboard backlog first (Tom) - 9 open requests from merchandising (constructed) - 3 weeks of Tom and Sam (constructed estimate) - cost of waiting: requests keep arriving by direct message

Route it to the decider from the session 2 table. Look up the row, do not negotiate it. If no row covers the decision, that gap is the real finding: add the row first, then use it.

★ Step 2 · route to the deciderDecision: priority order Decider: Ana (from the session 2 table, constructed) Consulted: Tom, Sam (from the table), plus Wei for this case Informed: stakeholders, and the team at the next stand-up Rule: decider decides after hearing both written cases

Set the time limit. A disagreement with no end date keeps leaking into every meeting. Pick a date close enough that waiting costs little, and say what happens if the decider is away.

★ Step 3 · time limitCases written by: Wednesday 17:00 Decision by: Friday 12:00 (constructed) If Ana is away: the deputy named in the table decides Until then: both keep their current work; nobody starts either option early

Record the decision, the dissent and the review date. The person who lost the argument should be able to see their case was heard and when it will be looked at again. Commit to the decision in the meantime; reopening it before the review date needs new information, not new volume.

★ Step 4 · decision log entryDecided: forecast rebuild first; dashboards next (constructed) Why: stock planning depends on it this quarter Dissent: Tom - merchandising requests will pile up; mitigation: Sam triages the 9 requests weekly Review: first Monday of next month Reopen if: request backlog passes 15 (constructed threshold)

Run a five-minute safety check in the retro. The questions below are Ana's own, written in plain words for Northline; they are not Edmondson's survey items, which were not read for these pages. Ask the same questions each time, anonymously, so the trend means something. The retro itself is part of the rhythm from session 3; running that meeting well is a facilitation craft, covered in Facilitation ↗.

★ Step 5 · Ana's constructed safety checkAnonymous, 1 to 5, same four questions every retro: 1. If I broke something, I would say so the same day. 2. I can say "I do not understand" without it costing me. 3. When I disagreed with a plan this sprint, it was heard. 4. Disagreements here stay about the work. Then 3 minutes: Ana reads the spread aloud, not the names, and asks one question: "what would make number 1 easier?"

Write down what Ana does when Kofi reports the broken table. The first response to a reported error is the one the whole team remembers. Decide it in advance, so it is the same every time and does not depend on how bad the week was.

★ Step 6 · when someone says "I broke it"1. Thank: "Thanks for saying it now - that saves us Monday." 2. Fix: who can repair it, by when; Kofi helps, Priya leads 3. Learn: 10 minutes at the next retro on HOW it got through (the change process), never WHO 4. No blame: the incident note names the step that failed, not the person who was standing on it

Test it on last month. Ana replays Kofi's broken table through step 6 and the Tom and Wei fight through steps 1 to 4 (constructed). The table would have surfaced on day one; the fight would have had a decider and a Friday deadline instead of three stand-ups of sniping. A protocol that would not have changed your last bad week is not finished yet.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Edmondson's 1999 study of 51 work teams find, according to its abstract?

The abstract links safety to learning behaviour and learning behaviour to performance; team efficacy did not carry the effect. No effect size is quoted on these pages, so B invents one, and D confuses safety with niceness.

2 · A colleague says "Google proved psychological safety is the only thing that matters for teams". What is the accurate description of the source?

The re:Work guide is real and specific, and it is a company summarising its internal study. It lists five dynamics, not one, and its methods are not published. C overcorrects: a company report is still evidence, just a different tier.

3 · What do the conflict meta-analyses by De Dreu and Weingart (2003) and de Wit, Greer and Jehn (2012) disagree about?

Relationship conflict is the stable part: both report it negative. The open question is task conflict, and the 2012 moderators (reported) suggest it is least harmful when kept apart from the personal kind. C states as proved what neither paper found.
