Learn Team Management with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

Who decides what

Most team arguments are not about the answer. They are about who gets to give it. When nobody knows, a request bounces from person to person until someone copies in the lead, and the lead ends up deciding things she knows least about. Tonight we list the decisions a team makes every month, give each one a single decider, split the calls you can walk back from the ones you cannot, and agree how a disagreement ends on a date instead of when the most stubborn person wins.

Part 1 · covers decision rights

Name one decider for every recurring decision

5 min live

A decision right is a sentence written down before the argument: for this kind of decision, this person decides, these people are asked first, and these people are told after. It does not make anyone more senior. It removes the question that slows a team down most, "is this mine to call?", and it sends each decision to the person closest to its consequences, who is very often not the lead.

LiveList the decisions before you argue about who makes them3 min▶

Start with the list, not the people. Ask the team: what did we decide last month, and what will we decide again next month? Priority order, a new request from a stakeholder, a change to a table other people read, retiring a dashboard, shipping a model, the on-call rota. Most teams find a short list, rarely more than a dozen, that comes back every month. One-off decisions do not need a row; recurring ones do, because they are the ones that keep restarting the same argument.

Then three columns per decision. Decider: exactly one name. Two names is a committee, and a committee of two decides when the less busy person gives in. Consulted: the people whose input the decider must hear before deciding, because the decision changes their work. Informed: the people told after, so nobody is surprised. Consulted means heard, not given a veto.

Pick the decider by consequence, not rank. Who lives with the result? Who knows the most about the cost of getting it wrong? At Northline, a change to the shared sales table breaks Priya's pipeline first if it goes wrong, so Priya decides it, after hearing Tom and Wei, whose work reads that table. Ana decides the weekly priority order, because trading one stakeholder's request against another's is her job. The lead decides the decisions that are genuinely about the whole team, and hands the rest down.

Self-studyDecide, consult, inform is not RACI2 min▶

A decision-rights row answers who makes a recurring decision for a standing team. RACI answers who does, approves, advises on and hears about each deliverable of a project, and is built and retired with the project; it is taught in Tech Project PMO ↗. The two can sit side by side: the team's table outlives every project the team runs.

Real world

The lead who decides everything. A common pattern in growing data teams: the lead started as the strongest engineer, so every question still goes to her. Table changes, dashboard colours, which test to run, who covers Friday. Each decision takes her a couple of minutes; there are dozens of them a week, and each one waits until she has a gap between meetings. The team is not slow because people are slow. It is slow because every decision has one queue. Writing the decision-rights table is usually the moment the lead discovers how many of those decisions she never needed to make.

Part 2 · covers reversible and hard-to-reverse decisions

Fast for the doors you can walk back through, slow for the ones you cannot

5 min live

Not every decision deserves the same process. A dashboard layout can be changed back next week if it is wrong; dropping a source system or changing a shared table's grain cannot, because other people build on it the moment it ships. Giving every decision a written proposal makes a team slow on the small things. Giving every decision a quick chat makes it careless on the big ones. Two speeds, chosen by one question, avoid both.

LiveTwo speeds, and what each one costs3 min▶

Reversible calls: one person, a short consult at most. A dashboard layout, the order of sections in a weekly report, which chart type to use. If it is wrong, it costs a week and a fix. The decider asks the people whose work it touches, decides the same day and tells the rest. Slowing these down buys nothing and teaches the team that every decision is a meeting.

Hard-to-reverse calls: a written proposal and a review window. Dropping a source system, changing a shared table's grain from daily to weekly, choosing a vendor the team will be locked into. Once shipped, other people's work starts depending on it, and walking back costs far more than deciding slowly. The decider writes one page (the change, the options considered, who it affects, how to roll back if possible), opens a review window, and decides when it closes. Northline sets the window at 48 hours (constructed); the right length is the one your team can actually read in.

Say which speed out loud. Most arguments about process are really two people assuming different speeds. "This is a reversible call, I will decide it today after hearing from you" ends that argument before it starts.

Self-studyWhere this idea comes from, and what the evidence says3 min▶

No single source. Splitting decisions by how easily they can be undone is common practice among engineering and product teams, written up in many company blogs and leadership books. No study read for these pages tests it, and no one author owns it, so it is taught here as a working habit with a clear reason, not as a finding.

What Google reports about clarity. Google's re:Work guide on team effectiveness lists five dynamics from its study of 180 internal teams, in this order: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, impact. Structure and clarity is the one closest to decision rights: whether people know their roles and how the team works. The guide is a company's summary of its own internal study, with methods and data not published, so read it as "Google reports", not as proof that a decision-rights table raises effectiveness. It does not test decision tables directly.

Real world

The quick change that was not quick. A familiar story: an engineer changes a shared table from one row per order to one row per order line, because a new report needs it. It looks like a small fix and ships the same afternoon. By the next morning every dashboard summing order values reads too high, a forecast model has trained on doubled numbers, and several teams are rebuilding from yesterday's backup. Nobody was careless. The change was treated as a reversible call because nobody had written down that grain changes are not.

Part 3 · covers closing disagreement

How a disagreement ends on time

5 min live

A decider does not stop people disagreeing, and should not. Disagreement on the work is how a team finds the flaw in a plan before it ships. What a team needs is an agreed way for the disagreement to end: a rule named before the discussion, a time limit, a fallback when the time is up, and a log that records the decision and the dissent so the people who lost the argument can still be right later.

LiveName the rule before the discussion starts3 min▶

Sam Kaner's Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making (2014, 3rd ed.) lists the decision rules a group can use: unanimous agreement, majority vote, flip a coin, person-in-charge decides after discussion, and person-in-charge decides without discussion. None is right for everything. The trap is starting a discussion without saying which one applies, so half the room thinks they are voting and the other half thinks they are advising.

For a team with a decision-rights table, the default for most rows is "the decider decides after discussion": everyone consulted is heard, then one person calls it. Some rows earn a different rule. Northline's working agreements, for example, are decided by majority vote in the retrospective, because an agreement imposed on a team is just a rule. How to run the discussion itself, so the options are heard well, belongs to Facilitation ↗.

LiveAn escalation path with a time limit, then commit3 min▶

The time limit. Northline's rule (constructed): the consult window is two working days. If the people consulted agree, good. If they do not, the decider decides at the end of day two anyway. Anyone who thinks the decision is badly wrong can escalate to Ana once, in writing, within the same window. The escalation does not reopen the discussion; it asks one question: did the decider follow the process and hear the people who had to be heard?

Commit, and log the dissent. Once the decision is made, everyone acts on it as if it were their own. That only works if disagreement was heard, so the decision log records it: what was decided, by whom, who disagreed and why, and the date to look again. Dissent in the log is not a grudge. It is the team's early-warning record: if the review date shows the dissenter was right, the team learns whose judgement to weight next time, and nobody has to say "I told you so".

Self-studyWhy "consensus" without a fallback hands the decision to the most stubborn person3 min▶

Consensus sounds like the fairest rule: nobody is overruled. In practice, a rule that needs everyone to agree gives every person a veto, and with no deadline the decision goes to whoever can hold out longest. That is usually not the person with the best argument; it is the person with the most patience, the least to lose from delay, or the most seniority. The people who give in first are often the ones who most needed a decision to get on with their work.

Google's re:Work guide lists consensus-driven decision making among the factors that were not significantly connected with team effectiveness in its study. That is a company report on its own teams, not a test of consensus against other rules, so it does not prove consensus is harmful. It only means that one company did not find it was what set its effective teams apart; the case against consensus without a fallback rests on the veto mechanism above, not on that report. Consensus with a fallback ("if we do not agree by Thursday, Priya decides") keeps the good part, everyone tries to agree, without the veto.

Real world

The month-long veto. A pattern many teams recognise: a team proud of deciding everything by consensus wants to move its reporting to a new tool. Everyone agrees except one senior analyst, whose reasons are partly sound and partly habit. Nobody wants to overrule a respected colleague, so the decision returns to every weekly meeting for a month. Meanwhile two people build workarounds in the old tool that will have to be migrated later. When the team finally adds a rule, "the platform owner decides after hearing objections", the decision takes one meeting, and the analyst's concerns become items on the migration checklist instead of a veto.

Build-along

Northline's decision-rights table, eight rows

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Ana builds Northline's table with the team in one session (constructed): eight recurring decisions, how reversible each one is, one decider, the people consulted and told, the closing rule, and the log. Write the same table for your own team as you go. Leave the decider column until last, so the conversation is about the decisions, not about status.

List the decisions the team makes every month. Look back over the last month of chat, tickets and meeting notes and write down every decision that came up more than once. Northline finds eleven and keeps eight; the other three were one-offs (constructed). Name each decision as a verb, so it is clear what is being decided.

★ Step 1 · the list1. Set the weekly priority order 2. Accept (or decline) a new stakeholder request 3. Change a shared table (columns, grain, meaning) 4. Retire a dashboard 5. Choose a model to ship 6. Set the on-call rota 7. Buy a tool under the team's tool budget 8. Change the team's working agreements Dropped as one-offs: office move, conference budget, the spring re-org question (constructed)

Mark each one reversible or hard to reverse. Use the Part 2 question: can it be walked back within a week without anyone else's work breaking? Argue about this column, not the decider column; it is the one that sets the process.

★ Step 2 · reversible or hardReversible (decide fast): priority order, stakeholder request, model to ship (only if the previous model stays ready to switch back), on-call rota, tool under budget, working agreements Hard to reverse (written proposal + 48h review): change a shared table -> dashboards and models read it retire a dashboard -> people build habits on it

Name one decider for each row. One name per row, chosen by who lives with the consequence. Check how many rows name the lead. Northline started with Ana on six of eight; after one pass she is on two (constructed). Every row she gives away is one fewer request that waits in her queue.

★ Step 3 · one decider eachPriority order Ana (trades one stakeholder off against another: her job) Stakeholder request Sam (runs the intake) Shared table Priya (owns the pipeline it lives in) Retire a dashboard Tom (built and maintains them) Model to ship Wei (owns the forecast model) On-call rota Kofi (runs the platform) Tool under budget Kofi Working agreements Ana, by team majority vote

Fill in consulted and informed. Consulted: whose work changes if this goes either way. Informed: who would be surprised to find out later. Keep consulted short; a long list turns into the consensus problem from Part 3.

★ Step 4 · consult and informShared table: consult Tom, Wei inform stakeholders Priority order: consult Tom, Sam inform stakeholders Model to ship: consult Lena, Priya inform Ana, stakeholders Consulted means heard before the decision, not a veto.

Write the closing rule and the fallback. One rule for the whole table, plus any row that is different. Put the time limit in writing, and the single escalation route.

★ Step 5 · how a disagreement endsDefault rule: the decider decides after discussion Exception: working agreements -> majority vote in retro Time limit: consult window = 2 working days (constructed) Fallback: no agreement at the end of day 2 -> the decider decides anyway Escalation: once, in writing, to Ana, within the window; she checks the process, not the answer After: everyone commits; dissent goes in the log

Start the decision log. One line per decision from the table, in a shared doc or a channel the whole team can search. This is the record that settles "I thought we agreed..." in ten seconds, and the place a dissenter can point to at review time.

★ Step 6 · decision log templateDate: 2026-10-12 Decision: Sales table moves from daily to weekly grain Row: change a shared table (hard to reverse) Decider: Priya Consulted: Tom (agreed), Wei (disagreed: model needs daily) Dissent: Wei - forecast accuracy may drop; keep a daily view for the model Review: 2026-11-12, check forecast accuracy report (all entries constructed)

Test it on the last two arguments. Ana replays both. Which dashboard to build next: row 2, Sam decides after hearing Ana, the finance and merchandising leads are told; a week of waiting becomes a day. Whether to change the shared table: row 3, Priya writes one page, Tom and Wei have two working days, Priya decides. Neither ends with Ana copied in. A table that cannot settle a recent argument needs another row.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. List your team's recurring decisions. Go back a month in chat and meeting notes. Keep the ones that came up more than once.
  2. Mark each reversible or hard to reverse, using the one-week question, and write the process each speed gets.
  3. Name one decider per row, then count your own name. If you are the decider on more than half the rows, pick two to hand to the person closest to the consequences.
  4. Start a decision log with the next real decision your team makes, including any dissent and a review date. Bring it to session 3.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does a row in a decision-rights table need?

One decider per row is the point: two names make a committee and a vote for everyone makes a veto. Consulted people are heard, not given a veto, and the decider is chosen by consequence, which is often not the lead.

2 · Priya wants to change the grain of a shared sales table; Tom wants to change the layout of one of his dashboards. How should the two decisions be handled?

The question is whether a decision can be walked back within a week without breaking anyone else's work. A layout can; a grain change breaks every dashboard and model that reads the table. Giving both the slow process makes small calls slow; giving both the fast one makes big calls careless.

3 · The two-day consult window on a shared-table change closes and Tom and Wei still disagree with Priya. What happens next?

The fallback is what makes the time limit real. Without it, consensus hands the decision to whoever holds out longest. Escalation to Ana checks that the process was followed; it does not move the decision to her.
