Where we start

Northline is constructed: seven invented people, a declared map of who knows which of seven work areas, and a set of rules for how a week is spent, all printed on the widget. That makes the bench honest about what it is. It cannot tell you how your team spends its week. It can show, on stated rules you can argue with, which way each design pushes the numbers and who in the team pays for it.

Part 1 · covers what the bench computes

How a week becomes numbers

6 min live

Every person starts with a 40-hour week. The bench takes four costs out of it and calls what is left focus time: rituals, coordination along each path the person sits on, handoffs across a team boundary, and interrupts. Two numbers are exact: the path count and the bus factor, both arithmetic on the declared team. Everything measured in hours is modelled.

LiveFive numbers to read any design3 min▶

Communication paths (exact): pairs inside each team, plus one for each declared interface between teams. Meeting hours per person (modelled): the ritual set only. A team of 7 spends 3.5 hours each a week in it; a team of 9 spends 4.0, because a stand-up and a planning meeting both grow with the people in them.

Focus hours per person (modelled): the week minus all four costs, averaged. Lowest individual focus: the person the design squeezes most, because an average can rise while one person sinks. Bus factor (exact on the declared map): the fewest departures that would leave some work area with nobody who knows it. Northline's is 1, and the one is Priya.

Self-studyWhat the rules leave out3 min▶

The model prices time, not quality. A ritual that saves no hours might still prevent the wrong dashboard from being built, and a split that adds focus might lose the conversation where Wei hears what Tom's stakeholders actually need. Neither shows up as an hour. The coordination rule assumes every path costs the same, when in a real team two or three paths carry most of the traffic. The interrupt draws are seeded, so the same numbers appear on every visit, which is what makes them checkable and also why they are not anyone's real week.

The bus factor here is the simple version: the smallest number of people who know any one area, on a map the team declares. Research versions estimate it from who wrote which files in a code base, and session 6 reads one of those studies.

Build-along · the bench

Four designs, a late project, and a resignation

★ 26 min · the main event

The bench opens on design 2, Northline with a weekly ritual set. Read it first: the five numbers, each person's week, the knowledge map and the comparison table. Then work through designs 1, 3 and 4, saying out loud what moved and who paid. Press the break button on design 2 and on design 4. Finish with the late project at 4 weeks, then drag the weeks.

Read it in this order. The tag, then the verdict line, then the lowest individual focus before the average. On design 2 the average is 31.9 focus hours, and the lowest is Ana at 29.8, because the lead carries the most interrupts. Every design moves both numbers, and they do not always move together.

Part 2 · covers what the four designs show

Each design buys something, and someone pays for it

6 min live

The numbers below are the ones the bench prints, written down so they can be argued with after class. An independent Python run of the same rules agrees with the browser on every value.

LiveDesign 1 to 2: the ritual set nearly breaks even2 min▶

With no shared rituals, each of Northline's 21 paths costs each of its two people 60 minutes a week of ad hoc syncing: 6.0 hours a person. The ritual set costs 3.5 hours a person and cuts the path cost to 20 minutes, 2.0 hours. Net, focus rises from 31.4 to 31.9 hours a person. Half an hour. Now drag the ad hoc slider on design 1 to 30 minutes: the team without rituals has 34.4 focus hours, and the ritual set costs 2.5 hours a person. A ritual set pays only when it replaces coordination that was really happening. Session 3 built the budget that tells you which case you are in.

LiveDesign 3: fewer paths, and a cost that moves to two people2 min▶

Splitting into pipeline and models (Priya, Wei, Lena, Kofi) and insights (Ana, Tom, Sam), joined by one interface between Wei and Tom, cuts the paths from 21 to 10 and lifts the average to 32.6 focus hours, the highest of the four designs. Both sub-teams' rituals are smaller. But the 6 items a week that cross between the teams each cost the two liaisons 40 minutes, and Tom drops to 29.8 hours and Wei to 30.5 while Lena and Kofi rise to 34.7 and 34.8. An interface is not free; it is a cost the design concentrates on whoever stands at it. Conway's law from session 1 adds the other half: the work the two teams produce will start to meet at that same interface.

Self-studyDesign 4: more capacity, less per person2 min▶

Nine people, after both hires are fully up to speed: 36 paths, 4.0 meeting hours each, and focus per person falls to 31.1 hours. Team focus rises from 223.1 to 279.6 hours, so the team can do more, but the two people added 56.5 hours, not the roughly 63.7 two of the original seven would bring, because every veteran now coordinates along two more paths. The design does buy one thing the hours do not show: Jun has learned the pipeline, so the bus factor is 2. Press the break button on design 4 and nothing stops.

Real world

The split that made a hero of the liaison. A common pattern when a growing data team divides into "platform" and "analytics": the person who knew both sides becomes the interface, every cross-team request routes through them, and within a quarter they are the busiest and least productive person on either team. The split was right; the interface was one person wide. The usual fix is more than one liaison, or a written contract for what crosses (session 6 has the async version), not undoing the split.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

Add two people late, and lose the one who knows

6 min live

The designs compare steady states. These two buttons are about change. One tests the most quoted rule in team management on the model's own terms; the other removes a person and shows what the declared knowledge map says stops.

LiveThe anti-lever: add two people to a late project2 min▶

The model: Northline with its ritual set has 223.1 focus hours a week and a fixed amount of work left. Two hires join on day one. They deliver nothing in weeks 1 and 2, a quarter of their focus in weeks 3 and 4, half in 5 and 6, three quarters in 7 and 8, then all of it. Each takes 6 hours a week of veteran time in weeks 1 to 4 and 3 hours in weeks 5 to 8, and the team of 9 has 36 paths and bigger rituals. With 4 weeks of work left, the team finishes in 4.2 weeks: later. By then the veterans have spent 54 hours mentoring and the hires have 259 hours that were not yet productive.

The honest nuance: drag the weeks. At 6 weeks left the hires still cost time (6.0 against 6.0, a few hours later); at 7 they save a little (6.9); at 20 they save over two and a half weeks (17.4). On these rules "adding people makes it later" is true near the deadline and false with time to spare. The crossover is a property of the ramp and mentoring numbers, which are invented. The shape is the lesson: new people cost before they pay, and how late the project is decides which side you are on.

LiveThe break button: Priya leaves2 min▶

A bench that can only report good news is measuring nothing. The break button removes Priya, the one person on the declared map who knows the pipeline. On designs 1, 2 and 3 the pipeline has nobody, the bus factor goes from 1 to 0, and 20 percent of Northline's weekly work stops. The table also shows the risk moving: infrastructure, which Priya shared with Kofi, now rests on Kofi alone. Paths fall from 21 to 15, and focus per person even rises slightly. The hours look better on the week she leaves, which is exactly why a team can miss the problem until the first pipeline failure.

On design 4, Jun has learned the pipeline. Press the button there: nothing stops, the bus factor goes from 2 to 1, and the pipeline now rests on Jun alone. One departure is survivable; the next is not. Session 6 turns that into a plan.

Compute your own bus factor tonight. List your team's work areas down the side and the people across the top, put a dot where someone could keep the area running for a month without help, and find the row with the fewest dots. That count is your bus factor, and that row is your first session 6 task.

Part 4 · covers Brooks's law and Conway, traced

"Adding people to a late project makes it later", traced

5 min live

The rule is usually quoted as a law of nature. It is an essay claim from experience, made in 1975 by someone who had run a very large software project, and it is better for being read as one.

LiveWhat Brooks argued, as reported2 min▶

Fred Brooks's The Mythical Man-Month (1975, anniversary edition 1995) is reported to give three reasons why adding people to a late software project makes it later: much of the work cannot be split into pieces people can do without talking to each other, new people take time to learn the project, and communication overhead grows as the team does. The chapter itself was not read for these pages, so the wording here is reported, and no later remark by Brooks about his own law is quoted.

Conway's 1968 paper, read at source, already made two of those points: possible communication paths grow at roughly half the square of the number of people, and it is a mistake to assume that adding manpower simply adds to productivity. The bench's anti-lever builds in all three reasons as stated rules and reports that they win near the deadline and lose with time to spare. That is consistent with the essay's own wording, which is about a project that is already late.

Self-studyHow to use the law without overusing it2 min▶

Quoted as "never add people", the law stops teams from hiring for work that is months away and genuinely needs more hands. Quoted as "people added late cost before they pay", it gives a lead a better question for a sponsor offering help: how many weeks are left, how long until a new person is useful here, and who will mentor them? If the honest answer to the second is longer than the first, decline the help and cut scope instead. Hiring itself, how to find and interview those people, is taught in AI HR ↗, and their first weeks in Performance Management, the onboarding ramp ↗.

The sentence to use with a sponsor offering two people. "Thank you. With four weeks left, two new people will cost us more in onboarding than they deliver before the date. If the date can move to the end of next quarter, they will pay for themselves; if it cannot, help me cut scope instead."

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, splitting Northline into 4 + 3 raises mean focus from 31.9 to 32.6 hours. What is the most complete reading?

The average rose, but Tom fell to 29.8 hours and Wei to 30.5 while Lena and Kofi rose to 34.7 and 34.8. A design concentrates cost on whoever stands at its interface. D mislabels a model as a measurement: Northline is constructed.

2 · On the bench's rules, two hires added with 4 weeks of work left finish in 4.2 weeks, and with 20 weeks left in 17.4. What is the honest statement?

Brooks's law is an essay claim about projects that are already late, and the model agrees in that region. A and B each take one side of a curve. C treats invented ramp numbers as a measurement.

3 · When Priya leaves design 2, focus per person rises slightly and paths fall from 21 to 15. Why is that not good news?

The hour figures only price time spent. The knowledge map shows what can no longer be done at all. That is why the break button exists: a bench that reports only better hours would miss the first pipeline failure.
